**SCREENTIME GUIDELINE**
================================================================
three key strategies:
1. Give simple countdown warnings 
  Use short warnings to prepare your child, such as: "5 minutes left", "2 minutes left", "Last one, then we stop". This helps your toddler adjust before the screen turns off.
2. Use a timer to make it neutral
  A visual timer or alarm shifts the boundary from you to the routine. It supports cooperation because the timer becomes the signal, not the parent.
3.  Redirect the child to a bridging activity and model it. Start playing the bridging activity and describe your actions to draw the child's attention. Do not pay attention to the child until they join you in the activity. Once they join, give labeled praise for their efforts.

Ways to mitigate emotion triggers.
1. Set a clear screen time routine
  Create consistent screen time routines so your child knows when screen time happens and when it ends. Predictability helps toddlers feel safe and reduces pushback.

2. Plan the transition before you turn it off (High Priority)
  Decide what comes next and tell your child ahead of time. A good screen time transition includes a positive next activity, such as blocks, colouring, snack time or going outside.

3. Choose a natural stopping point (High Priority)
  Whenever possible, stop at the end of an episode, level or song instead of turning it off midway. This makes the ending feel fair and easier to accept.

4. Give an effective command
  - Direct
  - Positively stated
  - Specific
  - One at a time
  - Developmentally appropriate
  - Normal tone of voice
  - Explanations provided before the command or after obeying the command
  Examples: "Turn off the TV." / "Hand me the phone." (It's OK to use "please".)

5. Effective follow-through
  - Praise: If your child begins to obey your command within five seconds, give labeled praise.
  - The Choice Flow: If ignored, did the parent offer a logical "This or That" choice? Is it age-timed? (Immediate for ages < 5; future privilege for ages >= 5.)
      Example: "You have two choices: either you turn off the TV, or I turn it off for you."
  - EXPECTED BEHAVIOR (Not Nagging): When a parent removes a privilege, they should make a brief, matter-of-fact statement connecting the behavior to the consequence. This is considered Effective Enforcement.
      Example: "You didn't do what I told you, so I turned it off for you."
  - NAGGING (Flag this): Repeatedly explaining the rule, pleading, or using long justifications while the child is refusing or after the consequence has been stated.
      Example: "Come on, why can't you just listen? We're going to be late and you know the rules..."

6. Co-regulate first, then teach
  If your child is upset, start with calm support. Name the feeling, stay close and hold the limit. This emotional co-regulation helps build emotional self-regulation over time, which is key to preventing future screen time meltdowns.

7. Stay consistent even if there is a protest (High Priority)
  If tantrums lead to extra screen time, the brain learns that protesting works. Keep the limit, stay calm, and repeat the routine. Consistency is what reduces meltdowns long term.

Progression:
  1. If the parent has done all of the above and it is still not working, introduce special play time.
  2. Once special play time reaches 80, suggest they use it as the bridging activity.
  3. Then ask the parent whether they want to practice discipline.
